# Privacy, relationships and responsible recipient profiles

Research brief • 31 October 2026

## Recommendation

Treat each proposed use of recipient information as a specific flow: who supplied it, whom it describes, who requests it, what can be used for the gift, and what can be shown or retained. A verified requester is identifiable, not automatically entitled to information. Default to the least revealing useful outcome, recipient control over shared facts, and a useful low-information experience when permission is absent. The controls below are design proposals to test, not findings that a particular interface or default is optimal.

Helen Nissenbaum's [contextual integrity framework](https://digitalcommons.law.uw.edu/wlr/vol79/iss1/10/) evaluates privacy by whether information collection and distribution fit the norms of a particular context. A memory shared with a personal assistant, a photo in a family group, and a public wish list have different contexts; moving any of them into a commercial gifting service changes the audience and purpose. A public source is not blanket permission to aggregate, infer, or republish. The framework motivates questions and testing; it does not itself establish a universal consent rule or validate these proposed controls.

## Audience and actual disclosure

Define audiences in plain language at the fact level, with a private preview of what each requester can see:

| Audience | Proposed meaning and default |
|---|---|
| Public | Anyone permitted by the product to access the public view; explain whether it is indexed or accessible without an account. Explicitly publish individual facts only. Public does not mean unrestricted harvesting or secondary use. |
| Connections | Recipient-approved connections, not anyone who asserts a relationship or knows a link. Define how connections are accepted, removed, and verified. |
| Trusted Circle | A recipient-selected subset of connections; membership is explicit and revocable. Do not assume all family, partners, or assistants belong. |
| Only Me | Recipient only, with service processing necessary to provide the chosen private feature; not shared with a giver or another assistant by default. |

Default a newly entered recipient fact to Only Me unless the recipient deliberately selects an audience; make the selected audience visible beside the fact. Visibility is a ceiling, not a command to send every eligible fact. At request time, authenticate the requester and evaluate current membership, purpose, relevance, fact-specific permission and whether even the recommendation would reveal the fact. Use current authorization for each request and deny or ask when identity, membership or delegation is uncertain. Links and assistant tokens must not bypass these checks. A recipient may narrow or withdraw access; access already seen, copied or independently retained cannot be recalled. Avoid promising instant deletion from external assistants or backups unless technically supported.

**Surprise-preserving request:** tell the recipient the verified requester's identity and that they seek gift guidance, without automatically revealing occasion, date, candidate or budget. Let the recipient approve a standing audience or a request, review a concise preview of the categories or facts, and choose deny, allow once or edit scope. The requester sees only the permitted result. An approval need not expose surprise details, though the request itself may signal a gift; offer an existing standing preference and a generic invitation to reduce that signal. Never imply approval from silence.

## Minimum useful profile with privacy gates

The minimum useful profile in [02](02-recipient-profiling.md) is a decision state, not a reason to build a dossier. Keep occasion, budget and deadline in the giver's request; persist a recipient fact only if it is needed for a defined purpose and permitted. An authorized current wish or concrete interest may be enough; use unknown and a general exploratory direction where it is not. Ask only for a constraint when it changes a candidate; if an essential safety constraint remains unknown, hold that candidate. A recipient can express “no scented products” or “avoid nuts” without supplying a diagnosis. Do not infer or store a health condition from that constraint.

For each retained claim store the minimum needed: claim, source or contributor, date or freshness, direct/observed/inferred status, qualitative confidence, purpose, visibility and permission state. Source identity itself can be sensitive; show a requester only a permitted attribution, e.g. “shared wish, updated recently,” and preserve fuller provenance in an appropriately restricted audit record. Keep conflicting claims distinct. A giver's private note is their observation, not a verified recipient fact or an automatic contribution to a shared recipient profile. Do not attach inferred personality, vulnerabilities, relationship quality, income or demographic stereotypes to a person. Month and day may support a birthday reminder if voluntarily supplied; do not collect birth year for that purpose. Month/day can still identify someone in context and needs the same controls. Age eligibility, where genuinely needed, is a separate question; do not infer it from a yearless birthday.

**Protected and high-risk information.** Do not solicit or infer medical diagnoses, disability, mental health, sex life or orientation, religion, political views, race or ethnicity, union membership, genetic or biometric identifiers, precise location history, financial hardship, account credentials, or secrets about another person as gift-profile inputs. This is a conservative product policy, broader than any one law. A direct, actionable exclusion can still be sensitive: minimize it, restrict its visibility, and do not disclose its underlying reason. If a user enters sensitive free text, offer local removal/redaction and prevent its onward sharing while reviewing how to handle it safely; automated detection alone cannot guarantee completeness. Do not transform a private fact into a revealing gift, explanation, filter, or refusal. If safe use cannot be assured, abstain and offer a general alternative.

## Assistants, third parties and disclosure

1. **Separate authority from access.** An assistant's ability to read a conversation or memory is not permission to export it. The recipient authorizes a specific assistant and purpose, with allowed facts or categories and audience. Multiple assistants receive separate, revocable grants; no assistant inherits another's access. Verify assistant identity and the human requester, use short-lived scoped credentials, and recheck grants before use and disclosure.
2. **Minimize the handoff.** Prefer an approved actionable preference over the original conversation, document, photo, transcript or broad memory search. An assistant should not transmit its private raw memories or infer a secret from them. A shared memory involving another person may reveal their information too: obtain appropriate permission from affected people or omit/redact it; one contributor's permission is not authority over everyone. Invite a non-identifying summary only where it remains safe.
3. **Separate processing from explanation.** Recipient authorization must cover use as well as audience. Send only necessary permitted context to the assistant doing the task, and only permitted, non-revealing reasons to the giver. A polished explanation, candidate title, search query, citation, refusal or comparison may leak a hidden fact. If the suggestion itself would expose it, suppress the suggestion; vague wording alone does not cure the leak. A generic fallback must not pretend that it has an evidence-based personal fit.
4. **Make rights and limits visible.** Show the recipient active assistant grants and recent disclosures, who received what category and when, with correction and revocation. Keep audit metadata minimal and access-controlled; do not put sensitive claim text into ordinary logs. Explain provider retention, subprocessors, training or other secondary uses before connection, and use enforceable provider settings and agreements. Do not claim that a provider deletes previously transmitted material unless confirmed.
5. **Purpose and retention.** Default request context and temporary handoffs to short, disclosed lifetimes; delete after the occasion or a defined inactivity period unless a person chooses to keep them. Keep an explicitly retained preference only for its declared purpose and review it periodically for staleness. Establish concrete schedules before launch for active data, logs, backups and vendors, with exceptions for security and legal obligations; execute deletion and track downstream requests. No advertising, model training, sale or unrelated profiling from recipient data without a separately evaluated lawful basis and clear, meaningful choice. This is a recommended product commitment, not an assertion of current implementation.

## Children and managed contacts

A parent or caregiver may be able to create a managed contact, but possession of a relationship label does not prove legal authority. Default managed profiles to private, minimal and non-public, use age-appropriate choices, restrict assistant and giver access, and make a path for the person to gain control or challenge a manager's authority when appropriate. Avoid public profiles, precise location, sensitive inferences and advertising-based profiling of children. Design for family conflict, abuse and guardianship changes; provide an assisted review route rather than automatically transferring data to a contested claimant. Where age or authority is unknown, limit collection and sharing until resolved. A managed contact created by an adult raises privacy and legal questions even when a particular children's rule does not apply.

The U.S. [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions) describes when COPPA applies, including child-directed services and general-audience services with actual knowledge of collecting personal information **from** children under 13; covered operators generally must provide notice and obtain verifiable parental consent before collection, subject to exceptions, and support parental review and deletion. It does not follow that every adult-entered record about a child automatically triggers COPPA; analyze the actual collection and service. The FTC's [2025 final-rule announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data) describes amended retention and disclosure requirements. Other jurisdictions and ages may impose additional obligations.

## Legal and operational boundaries

This is product research, not a jurisdiction-specific legal conclusion. For applicable EU/UK processing, identify the controller and processors, lawful basis for each purpose, cross-border transfers, rights, retention and security. Consent is a proposed product control; it is not the only possible legal basis and a giver cannot consent on another adult's behalf. GDPR Article 9 separately restricts processing of specified special categories and requires an applicable condition; minimization and purpose limitation also matter. See the [GDPR text, Articles 5, 6 and 9](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679). Do not assume a user's personal or household activity exempts a commercial service.

For third-party-sourced data, notice cannot automatically wait until after a surprise. The [UK ICO's Article 14 guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-be-informed/) says information is generally due within a reasonable period and no later than one month, or earlier at first communication or disclosure, subject to specific exceptions. A desire to preserve surprise is not itself a demonstrated exception. Determine applicable obligations and notice timing before launching a third-party profile; if no compliant, safe route exists, avoid collecting or disclosing the data. Also assess relevant state privacy law and children's law for intended markets with counsel.

## Critical reading and validation

[01](01-gifting-psychology.md) appropriately warns against demographic shortcuts and recommends authorized shared memories. Its gift experiments do not establish entitlement to gather personal information or transfer private conversations. [02](02-recipient-profiling.md) correctly separates recipient facts from giver context and treats freshness and permission as first-class; its proposed rich provenance, broad constraint categories and persistent profile themselves can expose sensitive data. Apply per-fact controls, restricted provenance and expiry, and avoid treating a giver's entry as recipient-authorized. [03](03-agent-recommendations.md) correctly cautions against revealing inferences and overclaiming revocation; its handoff is safe only if every assistant, downstream provider, query and resulting suggestion is within the recipient's authorization. These are operational requirements to verify, not capabilities established by recommendation research.

Test with consenting recipients and givers across relationships and sparse profiles: measure recipient understanding of the four audiences and actual disclosure, unwanted inferences, false authorization, revocation and correction success, surprise leakage, burden and gift usefulness. Include adversarial tests for removed connections, two assistants with different scopes, stale grants, a private allergy leaking through a recommendation, a shared memory naming a third person, and unknown guardian authority. Inspect real provider retention and deletion behavior. These evaluations test the proposals; neither contextual-integrity theory nor the prior briefs demonstrates product effectiveness.
